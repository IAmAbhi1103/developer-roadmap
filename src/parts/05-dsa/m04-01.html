Module 4 is months 10 to 12. You have seen every major pattern; now you make them reliable. The work changes from "learn something new" to "recall it fast, under pressure, with no help". You re-solve the problems that beat you, practise in contest conditions, train the interview habits (thinking out loud, testing, stating complexity), and, if you aim at top product companies, learn two extra range-query structures: the segment tree and the Fenwick tree.

This module also gives you three things to keep open while you practise: one table that maps a problem clue to a pattern, one page of templates you must be able to write from memory, and a short list of Java traps that cost marks in interviews. It connects back to every earlier module (Foundations, Core patterns, Advanced) and sideways to System Design, which is the other half of most product-company loops.

After this module you will be able to:

  • Run a revision system with a log and spaced repetition, and know which patterns are your weak spots.
  • Read a new problem and name the likely pattern in 5 to 10 minutes using the clue table.
  • Write binary search, lower bound, merge sort, partition, BFS, DFS, Dijkstra, union-find, topological sort and a basic DP from memory, in correct Java.
  • Run a 45-minute interview and an online assessment with a clear routine, and avoid the common Java gotchas.
  • Use company-tagged questions and contests sensibly, without chasing a rating.
  • Implement a segment tree and a Fenwick tree for range queries with updates, and choose between them and prefix sums.

The revision system: log, spaced repetition and re-solving

Solving a problem once proves little; keep a short log, re-solve every problem that needed help after 3 days, 2 weeks and 1 month, from a blank editor with a timer, and let the log show your weak patterns.

The most common mistake in months 10 to 12 is to keep chasing new problems. New problems feel productive, but memory fades fast: a pattern you met two months ago and never revisited is mostly gone. Interviews test recall under pressure, so the plan is to spend roughly half your time on revision and half on new problems (at least 15 new ones in this period, mostly in your weak areas).

Spaced repetition: 3 days, 2 weeks, 1 month

Memory decays quickly after learning, then slowly if you refresh it at growing gaps. For each problem marked needed hint, needed help or failed:

  1. Review 1, after 3 days. Re-solve from a blank editor. If you solve it cleanly, the next gap is 2 weeks.
  2. Review 2, after 2 weeks. Same rules. Clean solve means the next gap is 1 month.
  3. Review 3, after 1 month. A clean solve here means the problem is retired. A failure resets it to the 3-day step.

Problems you solved alone and quickly need no review. Do not review everything; review what hurt.

How to re-solve

  • Blank editor. No IDE hints, no copy-paste, no looking at the old solution. A plain text editor or the LeetCode box is fine.
  • Timed. Set 30 to 40 minutes for a medium, 15 to 20 for an easy. Stop the clock when it passes your own tests.
  • Out loud. Say the pattern, the idea, the complexity, as in an interview.
  • Compare after. Only now look at your old solution or the editorial. Note what you missed and update the key-idea cell.
  • Do not just reread. Reading a solution feels like knowing it. Writing it from nothing is the real test.

Using NeetCode 150 and the Striver sheet as checklists

Curated lists such as the NeetCode 150 and Striver's DSA sheet are good checklists: they cover every pattern in a sensible order, so you can see which topics you skipped. Use them like this: tick a problem only when you can re-solve it cold; in the last months, scan the list for unticked items and for topics where your log shows weakness. Do not try to finish a list for the sake of finishing; a list is a map, not a goal. The roadmap target is about 300 problems in your log by the end, which a list plus your own gaps and company questions covers comfortably.

A weekly rhythm that works

  • Mon, Tue, Thu: new problems (2 per session), then log them.
  • Wed: re-solve the problems due that day.
  • Fri: one timed mock (two mediums in 70 minutes, out loud).
  • Sat: weekly contest, then upsolve (see contests).
  • Sun: weak-pattern review and planning next week.

Common mistakes

  • Looking at the editorial after ten minutes. Struggle for 25 to 30 minutes first, then take a small hint, then the full solution.
  • Logging only successes. The failures are the useful rows.
  • Re-solving in the same editor tab where the old code is still visible.

The pattern-recognition cheat sheet: problem clue to pattern

Most medium problems announce their pattern in the first two sentences: the input shape, the word in the question, and the size of n; use this table to turn each clue into the first pattern to try.

Reading a problem, ask three questions in this order. (1) What is the input? sorted array, string, tree, graph, grid, intervals. (2) What is asked? a min/max, a count, a yes/no, all solutions, an order. (3) How big is n? The constraint tells you the allowed complexity (n ≤ 20 means exponential is fine; n ≤ 105 means O(n log n); n ≤ 109 means O(log n) or maths). See constraints to complexity. Choosing between two close patterns

  • Sliding window vs prefix sum: all numbers positive and you want the shortest/longest window: sliding window. Negatives present, or "sum equals k": prefix sum with a HashMap.
  • BFS vs Dijkstra: all edges cost the same: BFS. Different non-negative weights: Dijkstra.
  • Greedy vs DP: if a choice can be undone later by a better combination (coin change with odd coins), you need DP. If sorting plus a local rule is provably safe (activity selection), greedy works.
  • Backtracking vs DP: asked for all solutions: backtracking. Asked for the best value or the count: DP, if subproblems repeat.
  • DSU vs DFS for components: a static graph: either. Edges arriving over time ("after each union"): DSU.

When no clue fits

Write the brute force, then ask "what work is repeated?". Repeated lookups: hash map. Repeated range sums: prefix sums. Repeated subproblems: DP. Repeated scans over a sorted order: two pointers or binary search. Repeated min/max of a moving set: heap or deque.

Interview angle

Say the clue aloud: "the array is sorted and I need a pair, so two pointers or binary search". Naming the clue shows pattern skill, which interviewers value more than a lucky final answer.

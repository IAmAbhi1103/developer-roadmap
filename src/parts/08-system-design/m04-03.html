Availability, reliability and what "99.9% uptime" means

Availability is the fraction of time a system works; each extra "nine" cuts the allowed downtime ten times, components in series multiply their availabilities (worse), components in parallel multiply their failure chances (better), and you reach high availability by removing single points of failure with redundancy and failover.

Availability as a percentage

Availability = uptime ÷ (uptime + downtime), usually written as a percentage and described by its "nines". "Three nines" means 99.9%. The allowed downtime shrinks very fast:

AvailabilityNinesDowntime per yearPer month (30 days)
99%twoabout 3.65 daysabout 7.2 hours
99.9%threeabout 8.77 hoursabout 43 minutes
99.99%fourabout 52.6 minutesabout 4.3 minutes
99.999%fiveabout 5.26 minutesabout 26 seconds

The formula is downtime = (1 − availability) × period. For 99.9% over a year: 0.001 × 365 days × 24 h = 8.76 hours. The jump from 99.9% to 99.99% looks small but means going from "a bad afternoon per year" to "less than an hour per year", which usually needs automatic failover, because no human can react in minutes. Each nine costs much more than the last, so choose the number from the business need. A college timetable app does not need five nines; a payments switch does.

SLI, SLO and SLA

SLI (Service Level Indicator)
A measured number: "the fraction of requests that returned non-5xx", "the share of requests under 300 ms".
SLO (Service Level Objective)
The internal target for an SLI: "99.9% of requests succeed over 30 days". Teams use it to decide when to stop shipping features and fix reliability.
SLA (Service Level Agreement)
A contract with customers, often with refunds or credits when broken: "99.95% monthly uptime or you get a credit". The SLA is looser than the SLO so the team has a safety margin.

The gap between 100% and your SLO is the error budget. With a 99.9% SLO you may fail 0.1% of requests; if the budget is spent, you pause risky releases. This turns "be reliable" into a number that engineers and product managers can argue about calmly.

Availability in series and in parallel

A request usually passes through many components. How they are connected decides the total.

In series (every part is required)

Multiply the availabilities. The chain is weaker than its weakest link:

Total = A1 × A2 × A3
Load balancer 99.9% × app 99.9% × database 99.9%
  = 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 0.997  →  99.7%
Downtime ≈ 0.003 × 8,760 h ≈ 26 hours per year (not 8.77 hours!)

Ten dependencies at 99.9% each give about 99%. This is why a microservice call chain of ten services is dangerous: the more hard dependencies a request needs, the lower the total availability.

In parallel (any one part is enough)

Multiply the failure chances. The system is down only if all copies are down:

Total = 1 − (1 − A1) × (1 − A2)
Two servers at 99.9% each
  = 1 − (0.001 × 0.001) = 1 − 0.000001 = 0.999999  →  99.9999%
Three servers: 1 − 0.001^3 = 99.9999999%

These are theoretical best cases. They assume failures are independent. If both servers share one power supply, one rack, one bug or one bad deploy, they fail together and the calculation is wrong. This is why real redundancy spreads copies over racks, availability zones and regions, and why code bugs (identical on every copy) are not fixed by adding replicas.

Combining both

Compute parallel groups first, then multiply the groups in series. Example: load balancer (99.99%) → two app servers (99.9% each, parallel) → one database (99.9%): app group = 99.9999%, total = 0.9999 × 0.999999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.89%. The single database is now the weak point. You improve the system by making that part redundant, not by buying more app servers.

Single points of failure and redundancy

A single point of failure (SPOF) is any component whose failure takes the whole system down. Walk the request path and ask of every box: "if this dies, what happens?" Typical SPOFs: one load balancer, one database primary, one DNS provider, one availability zone, one shared config service, one person who knows the password. The cure is redundancy: run more than one copy and make the switch between them automatic.

Failover: active-passive vs active-active

Active-passive (standby)Active-active
HowOne node serves; a standby waits and takes over on failureAll nodes serve traffic at once
Failover timeSeconds to minutes (detect, promote, redirect)Near zero; others already serving
Capacity useStandby idle: wasted moneyAll capacity used
ComplexitySimpler; one writerHigher: shared state, conflicts, split-brain risk
ExampleDatabase primary with a hot standbyStateless app servers behind a load balancer

Failover steps in a typical active-passive pair: a health check or heartbeat detects the primary is down, a coordinator promotes the standby, traffic is redirected (virtual IP, DNS or the load balancer), and the old primary is fenced off so it cannot keep accepting writes. Split-brain is the danger case: both nodes believe they are primary and accept different writes. Quorum-based election (an odd number of voters, majority wins) and fencing prevent it. Also note that async replication may lose the last few writes at failover (Replication).

Redundancy can go beyond servers: multiple availability zones (separate buildings in a city) survive a data centre fire; multiple regions survive a regional outage and also reduce latency, at the cost of complexity and money.

Reliability, availability, durability, fault tolerance

These four words are often mixed up.

TermMeaningExample
ReliabilityThe system does the correct thing, consistently, over timeA transfer debits and credits the right amounts
AvailabilityThe system is up and answers when askedThe app responds 99.95% of the time
DurabilitySaved data is not lostS3 advertises extremely high durability (eleven nines); a single disk has far less
Fault toleranceThe system keeps working while parts failOne of three replicas dies, users notice nothing

They are different: a system can be available but unreliable (it answers quickly with wrong data), or durable but unavailable (data is safe, but the site is down for an hour). A fault is a component misbehaving; a failure is when users see the effect. Fault tolerance means faults do not become failures.

Techniques to raise availability

  • Redundancy at every layer, spread across zones.
  • Health checks and automatic failover, so recovery is in seconds.
  • Timeouts, retries with backoff and jitter, and circuit breakers so one slow dependency does not drag down the caller (Module 6).
  • Graceful degradation: when recommendations are down, show the page without them rather than an error.
  • Safe deployments: rolling, blue-green or canary releases and quick rollback; many outages come from changes, not hardware.
  • Backups and tested restore; monitoring and alerts on the SLIs.

Exception: "available" does not mean "correct"

A server that is up but answers with stale or wrong data counts as available in the simple formula. That is why SLIs should measure successful responses (right status, within the latency target), not just whether the process is alive.

Common mistakes

  • Adding a second app server but keeping one database and calling it highly available.
  • Ignoring that serial dependencies multiply: five hard dependencies at 99.9% is about 99.5%.
  • Promising 99.999% in an SLA without automatic failover or an on-call process.
  • Counting planned maintenance as "not downtime" without telling users.

Interview angle

  • "99.9% is about 8.8 hours of downtime per year, 43 minutes per month. Four nines is about 52 minutes per year, which needs automatic failover."
  • Show the arithmetic: serial 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.8%; parallel 1 − 0.0012 = 99.9999%.
  • Say "no single point of failure" and then name each layer's redundancy: multi-AZ load balancer, N app instances, database with a standby, replicated cache.

CAP theorem and PACELC

CAP says that when a network partition happens, a distributed data system must choose between consistency (reject or delay requests) and availability (answer, maybe with stale data); since partitions cannot be avoided, the real choice is CP or AP, and PACELC adds that even without a partition you trade latency for consistency.

The three letters

  • Consistency (C): every read sees the most recent write, as if there were one copy of the data. (This is the "linearizable" meaning, not the same as the "C" in ACID.)
  • Availability (A): every request to a working node gets a non-error answer, without a guarantee it is the latest data.
  • Partition tolerance (P): the system keeps operating even when the network drops or delays messages between nodes, so the cluster splits into groups that cannot talk.

The partition scenario

Two data centres, Mumbai and Chennai, each hold a copy of a user's balance: ₹1,000. The link between them breaks. Both sides are still running and still receiving requests. A user in Mumbai withdraws ₹800. Mumbai cannot tell Chennai. Now a request for the balance arrives in Chennai. What should Chennai do?

  • CP (consistency over availability): Chennai refuses to answer (returns an error or waits) because it cannot be sure it has the latest value. The data stays correct; some users see errors until the partition heals.
  • AP (availability over consistency): Chennai answers ₹1,000 from its own copy. Everyone gets an answer, but it is stale, and if both sides accept writes the copies diverge and must be merged later.

When the partition heals, an AP system has to reconcile conflicting writes (last-write-wins by timestamp, version vectors, application-level merge, CRDTs); a CP system simply resumes.

Why "CA" is not a real choice

The folk version says "pick any two of three". That is misleading. In a distributed system over a network, partitions will happen (cables, switches, GC pauses, misconfigured firewalls look like partitions). You cannot choose to not have them. So P is not optional, and the real choice, during a partition, is between C and A. A "CA" system only exists on a single node (or one that is never partitioned), which is not distributed in the interesting sense. When nothing is partitioned, a system can offer both C and A; CAP only forces the trade-off during the partition.

Many systems are tunable

Cassandra and DynamoDB let each request choose how many replicas must agree (see quorums), so the same database can behave CP-like for one query and AP-like for another. Treat the labels as tendencies, not permanent tags. Behaviour also depends on configuration, so say "by default" or "when configured with…" in interviews.

PACELC: the full picture

CAP only talks about the rare partition. PACELC (Daniel Abadi) says: if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else (normal operation), choose Latency or Consistency. The second half matters every day: to keep replicas consistent, a write must wait for acknowledgements from other nodes (more latency); to be fast, it returns early and lets replicas catch up (weaker consistency).

System typeIf partitionElseExample
PA / ELAvailabilityLatencyCassandra and DynamoDB at default settings
PC / ECConsistencyConsistencyTraditional single-primary RDBMS with sync replication, Google Spanner-style systems
PC / ELConsistencyLatencySome designs that are strict only when partitioned

Cross-region replication shows the ELC trade-off clearly: a synchronous write to Singapore from Mumbai pays about 60 ms or more per write; asynchronous replication is fast but a regional failure can lose the last writes.

Choosing per feature, not per system

One product has different needs in different places. A strong design says so:

  • Payments, wallet balance, seat booking, inventory at checkout: choose consistency. An error is better than selling the same seat twice or losing money.
  • Likes, view counts, follower counts, feed ordering: choose availability and latency. Showing 1,203 instead of 1,205 hurts nobody.
  • Shopping cart: classic AP (Amazon's Dynamo paper): never refuse an "add to cart"; merge carts later.
  • User profile read right after update: needs at least read-your-writes, even in an otherwise eventual system.

Common mistakes

  • Saying "we pick CA". Distributed systems cannot opt out of partitions.
  • Treating CAP's "consistency" as ACID's "consistency". They are different ideas.
  • Calling a whole database "CP" or "AP" forever, instead of saying it depends on configuration.
  • Forgetting that partial failures (slow nodes, packet loss) behave like partitions.

Interview angle

  • Start from the partition: "When the network splits, I must either reject some requests (CP) or serve possibly stale data (AP)."
  • Add PACELC: "Even without a partition, stronger consistency costs latency."
  • Give a feature-level answer: "Payments are CP; the like counter is AP."

Design a ride-sharing app (like Uber or Ola)

Ride sharing needs a fast geospatial index of live driver locations (geohash cells or a quadtree), a matching service that offers the trip to nearby drivers with timeouts and atomic assignment, a trip state machine, and a real-time channel that streams the driver's position to the rider.

1. Requirements

Functional:

  • Rider: enter pickup and drop, see fare estimate and nearby vehicles, request a ride, see the driver arrive on the map, pay, rate.
  • Driver: go online, receive a request, accept or decline, navigate, start and end the trip.
  • Platform: match riders to drivers, compute ETA and fare, handle cancellations.

Non-functional: match within a few seconds; location updates in near real time (the car on the map should lag by seconds, not a minute); high availability in each city; a driver must never be assigned two rides at once, and a ride must never be given to two drivers (consistency for assignment); scalability to hundreds of thousands of online drivers. Out of scope: the routing engine, surge pricing model, fraud detection (mention briefly).

2. Capacity estimates (assumptions)

  • 20 million rides per day → 20,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 230 per second on average; peak 3× ≈ 700 ride requests per second.
  • 1 million drivers online at peak. Each sends its location every 4 seconds → 1,000,000 ÷ 4 = 250,000 location updates per second. Each update ~50 B → 12.5 MB/s. This is the dominant load; it is write-heavy and overwrites the previous value.
  • Location store size: 1,000,000 drivers × 100 B = 100 MB of latest positions. It fits in memory on a single machine; throughput, not size, forces sharding (split by city or by geohash prefix).
  • Nearby-driver reads: each ride request queries nearby drivers a few times, and riders opening the app also see nearby cars (say 10 map refreshes per ride request) → around 700 × 10 = 7,000 geo queries per second. Moderate.
  • Trip records: 20M × 2 KB = 40 GB/day. GPS trail kept for disputes: 20-minute trips sampled every 4 s = 300 points × 30 B = 9 KB per trip → 20M × 9 KB = 180 GB/day, stored cheaply (log store or object storage) and kept for a limited time.
  • Concurrent trips in progress: 700/s × average trip 20 min (1,200 s) ≈ 840,000 trips at peak, each streaming a position to a rider every few seconds → about 200,000+ messages per second to riders.

4. Data model

  • Rides/trips, riders, drivers, payments: relational, with strong consistency, sharded by city or ride id. Trip rows carry the state machine status and timestamps.
  • Live driver locations: an in-memory geo index (Redis GEO or a custom service) holding only the latest point per driver with a TTL (a driver who stops updating disappears after 30 seconds).
  • Location history: append-only into Kafka, then to cold storage for analytics, disputes and ETA model training.

5. High-level architecture

6. Deep dive A: geospatial indexing (geohash vs quadtree)

The question "which drivers are within 2 km of this point?" cannot use a plain index on latitude and longitude, since that checks one coordinate at a time and scans a huge band. We need a structure that maps 2D space into something searchable.

Geohash. A geohash encodes a latitude/longitude pair into a short string by repeatedly halving the world (alternating longitude and latitude bits) and writing the bits in base 32. Longer strings mean smaller cells, and cells that share a prefix are near each other. Approximate cell sizes:

Geohash lengthCell size (approx.)Use
5about 4.9 km × 4.9 kmCoarse area, city districts
6about 1.2 km × 0.6 kmTypical "nearby drivers" bucket
7about 150 m × 150 mFine pickup points

Index: keep a map from geohash (length 6) to the set of available drivers in that cell. To find drivers near a rider: compute the rider's cell and its 8 neighbours (a driver just across a cell edge has a different prefix, so neighbours are required), union their sets, then compute exact distance and filter by radius. Updating a driver: if the cell changed since the last update, remove from the old set and add to the new one. A geohash is also a good shard key: partition drivers by a coarse prefix so each shard handles one area.

Quadtree. A tree where each node covers a rectangle. If a node has more than N drivers (say 500), split it into four children (NW, NE, SW, SE). Dense downtown areas get deep, small cells; empty suburbs stay as big cells. To search, walk down to the leaf containing the point, then check neighbouring leaves until enough drivers are found. It adapts to density, unlike fixed-size geohash cells, but is harder to update at 250,000 moves per second and to distribute across servers (usually rebuilt in memory and partitioned by region).

AspectGeohash gridQuadtree
StructureFixed-size cells, string keysAdaptive tree
Update costCheap: change a set if the cell changedMove between leaves, maybe split/merge
Density handlingPoor: crowded cells are big setsGood: splits where crowded
DistributionEasy: shard by prefix; works in RedisHarder
Edge casesMust query neighbouring cellsMust query neighbouring leaves

Most practical answers use a geohash-style or hex grid (Uber published the H3 hexagonal grid for this) kept in memory; mention both and pick one with a reason: "geohash in Redis, simple and shardable".

7. Deep dive B: location updates at 250,000 per second

  • Reduce the rate. Update every 4 seconds while available or on a trip, every 15–30 seconds when idle. Send only when the driver moved more than a few metres. Adaptive rates cut the load a lot.
  • Writes go to memory. The location service overwrites the driver's entry in a sharded in-memory store; it does not touch the SQL database. Redis GEO (GEOADD) or per-cell sets work; shard by city or geohash prefix so each node takes tens of thousands of writes per second.
  • Persistence is secondary. If the in-memory store is lost, drivers re-send within 4 seconds, so it rebuilds itself. Meanwhile, a copy of every update goes to Kafka for history.
  • Staleness. Use a TTL or timestamp check; ignore drivers whose last update is older than ~15–30 seconds when matching.
  • Protocol. A persistent connection (WebSocket, MQTT) avoids repeated TLS handshakes and headers for a tiny payload; HTTP works at smaller scale.

8. Deep dive C: matching, assignment and the trip lifecycle

  1. The ride service stores the ride as SEARCHING and calls the matching service with pickup, type and time.
  2. Matching asks the geo index for available drivers of the right type within a starting radius (for example 2 km); if too few, widen it step by step.
  3. Rank candidates by road ETA, not straight-line distance (a driver 500 m away across a river may be 15 minutes away), using the routing service on the top 10–20 candidates only because routing is expensive. Also consider rating, acceptance rate and fairness.
  4. Offer the ride to the best driver with a 10–15 second timeout. Alternatives: offer sequentially (simple, slower) or to the top 3 at once (faster, wastes some driver attention).
  5. On acceptance, assign atomically: UPDATE ride SET driver_id = ?, status = 'ASSIGNED' WHERE ride_id = ? AND driver_id IS NULL and set the driver to ON_TRIP in the same transaction, or use a short Redis lease on the driver while the offer is open. One row updated means success; zero means someone else won the race (Coordination).
  6. If no driver accepts after a few rounds, widen the search; after a limit (say 2 minutes), tell the rider and cancel.
  • Real-time updates. The rider's app keeps a WebSocket to a realtime gateway (Chat describes the gateway and session registry pattern). During ASSIGNED and STARTED, the driver's updates are republished to the rider's channel every 2–4 seconds, and the app animates the car between points. Status changes (ASSIGNED, ARRIVED) are pushed too, with a push notification as a backup.
  • Fare. Estimate before the ride (distance × rate + time × rate + base + surge multiplier), compute the final fare from the actual trip. Surge: compare demand (requests) and supply (drivers) per cell over a sliding window and raise a multiplier; smooth it to avoid flapping.
  • Payment at trip end uses an idempotent charge (Payments).

9. Bottlenecks, scaling and failure

  • City sharding: riders and drivers in one city rarely interact with another city, so shard the geo index, matching and ride data by city (or region). A city outage then affects only that city.
  • Hot spots: airports and stadium exits have thousands of requests in a small area. Use finer cells there and queue requests per pickup zone.
  • Driver app offline: if heartbeats stop mid-trip, keep the trip alive, store the trail on the phone and upload later; notify support after a long gap.
  • Duplicates: accept and end calls carry idempotency keys; transitions are conditional.
  • Monitoring: time to match, offer accept rate, location staleness, cancellations by reason, geo query latency.

Follow-up questions

  • How do you handle a driver near a geohash cell boundary? (Search the 8 neighbouring cells too.)
  • Why not store driver positions in the SQL database? (250,000 writes per second of overwrites; memory store fits better; durability not needed.)
  • How do you pool shared rides? (Extend matching to combine requests with compatible routes and time windows; more complex optimisation.)
  • What if two riders are matched to one driver at the same instant? (Conditional update; the loser re-matches.)
  • How do you compute ETA accurately? (Road graph with live traffic, learned corrections, cached for popular segments.)

Design a video streaming platform (like YouTube)

A video platform splits into an upload pipeline (upload to object storage, transcode into many resolutions and small segments in parallel, publish) and a playback path (CDN serving adaptive-bitrate segments chosen by the player), because storage and bandwidth, not request counts, dominate the design.

1. Requirements

Functional:

  • Users upload videos (large files) with title, description and thumbnail.
  • Viewers search, open a video and watch with smooth playback on phones, laptops and TVs, on fast and slow networks.
  • Seek anywhere in the video; the video starts quickly.
  • View counts, likes and comments (basic). Recommendations are out of scope.

Non-functional: very high availability for playback; start-up delay of about a second or two; no buffering on stable networks; uploads must be reliable and resumable; uploaded videos must not be lost (durability); cost efficiency (storage and bandwidth are the bill); eventual consistency is fine for counts and for "video is processing" delays. Out of scope: live streaming, DRM details, recommendation models.

2. Capacity estimates (assumptions)

  • 100 million daily active users watching 5 videos each → 500 million views per day → 500,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 video starts per second.
  • Average watch time 5 minutes (300 s). Total viewing per day = 500M × 300 s = 150 billion seconds. Divide by 86,400 seconds in a day: average concurrent streams = 150,000,000,000 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 1.7 million; peak 3× ≈ 5 million.
  • Average stream bitrate 2.5 Mbps (megabits per second) → 2.5 ÷ 8 ≈ 0.31 MB/s. Peak egress = 5,000,000 × 2.5 Mbps = 12,500,000 Mbps = 12.5 Tbps (about 1.6 TB per second). No origin can serve this; the CDN must carry nearly all of it.
  • Uploads: 100,000 new videos per day → about 1.2 per second. Average raw size 500 MB → 100,000 × 500 MB = 50 TB per day raw, about 18 PB per year.
  • Transcoded versions: say 6 renditions (240p to 1080p) with the total close to the raw size → another ~50 TB per day, so roughly 100 TB per day, ~36 PB per year of video storage (approximate; codecs, lengths and ladders change this a lot). Object storage with tiering (hot, cold) is the only option.
  • Metadata: 100,000 videos × 2 KB = 200 MB per day. Tiny. Views: 5,800 counter increments per second, handled by batching.

Conclusion: request QPS is modest; bytes (storage and egress) and compute for transcoding drive the architecture.

The video bytes never pass through the API servers. The client uploads in parts straight to object storage with a pre-signed URL, which gives resumable uploads (retry only the failed part) and keeps the application tier light.

4. Data model

  • Metadata (videos, channels, comments): SQL or a document store; read-heavy and cached. Videos table: video_id, owner_id, title, description, status, duration, created_at with status UPLOADING, PROCESSING, READY, FAILED.
  • Video files and segments: object storage. Layout: raw/<id>/original.mp4 and vod/<id>/<resolution>/seg_00001.ts (or .m4s), plus manifests.
  • Search: an inverted index (Elasticsearch/OpenSearch) over titles, descriptions and tags, updated from a stream of metadata changes.
  • Counters: view and like counts aggregated from events; stored in a counter table or Redis and flushed periodically.

5. High-level architecture

6. Deep dive A: the upload and transcoding pipeline

Raw uploads come in many formats, sizes and bitrates. Transcoding turns each into a set of standard versions so every device can play something suitable. It is CPU-heavy, so it is done in parallel by many workers.

  1. Upload complete: an object-storage event (or the complete call) puts a job on a queue and sets status PROCESSING.
  2. Validate: check the file type, duration limits, run a virus/abuse and copyright scan (fingerprint matching), and extract metadata.
  3. Split the source into chunks of a few seconds on keyframe boundaries (groups of pictures). Each chunk can be encoded independently, so a long video is processed in parallel.
  4. Encode each chunk into every rendition of the bitrate ladder (for example 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, 1080p, optionally 4K) with a modern codec (H.264 is the universal baseline; VP9/AV1/HEVC give better compression at higher compute cost). This is a DAG of tasks: split → encode chunk × rendition → merge → package.
  5. Package into segments of 2–6 seconds and write a manifest: HLS uses .m3u8 playlists, MPEG-DASH uses .mpd. The master manifest lists the available renditions and their bandwidth.
  6. Extras: thumbnails and preview sprites, captions, audio tracks.
  7. Publish: write segments to the encoded bucket, set status READY, index it for search, and notify the uploader (Notifications).
  • Make every task idempotent (output path derived from video id, rendition and chunk number, so a retried task overwrites the same file) and retry failed chunks only (Retries). Use a workflow/orchestrator to track the DAG state (Orchestration).
  • Use spot or preemptible compute for workers, since tasks are retryable, to save cost.
  • Prioritise: short videos and new uploads from verified creators first; a backlog metric shows when to add workers.
  • Encode popular renditions first (so playback can start sooner) and add the rest later; or encode lazily the rarely used ones on demand.

7. Deep dive B: adaptive bitrate streaming and the CDN

Adaptive bitrate (ABR) streaming. The video is available in several renditions, each cut into short segments. The player downloads the manifest, starts with a medium rendition, and downloads segment after segment over plain HTTP. It measures download speed and buffer level; if the network slows, it switches to a lower rendition for the next segment, and switches back up when the network recovers. The user sees quality change, not buffering. Seeking is easy: compute the segment number from the time (time ÷ segment length), and fetch from there. Because segments are ordinary files over HTTP, they are cacheable by any CDN.

#EXTM3U
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=800000,RESOLUTION=640x360
360p/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=2800000,RESOLUTION=1280x720
720p/index.m3u8
#EXT-X-STREAM-INF:BANDWIDTH=5000000,RESOLUTION=1920x1080
1080p/index.m3u8

CDN. Playback traffic (12.5 Tbps at peak in our estimate) is served by edge servers near viewers (Module 5). Segments are immutable, so they cache perfectly with long TTLs. Design points:

  • Pull CDN with an origin shield: an edge miss asks a mid-tier cache, which asks the origin only if it also misses. This collapses many edge misses into one origin read.
  • Popularity is skewed: a small share of videos gets most views, so edge caches hold the popular head, and the long tail is fetched from origin storage on demand. For an expected hit (new release), pre-warm (push) segments to edges.
  • Multiple CDNs or ISP-embedded caches for cost and resilience; steer clients by DNS or manifest URLs.
  • Cost: egress dominates the bill, so a high cache hit ratio directly lowers cost. Efficient codecs reduce bytes per second.
  • Security: signed, expiring URLs for private or paid content; hotlink protection.

8. Deep dive C: metadata, search, counters and consistency

  • Metadata path. The video page API reads the cached metadata (Redis) and returns the manifest URL. Metadata is small, so a replicated SQL database with a cache is enough; shard by video id if needed.
  • View counts. 5,800 starts per second would hammer one row per popular video. Players emit view events into a stream; consumers aggregate in windows and add to counters periodically. Counts are approximate and a few minutes late; dedupe or filter bot traffic. Show rounded numbers in the UI.
  • Search. Index text fields; rank by relevance and engagement. The index is updated asynchronously from the metadata change stream (CDC, Outbox and CDC), so a new video becomes searchable after a short delay.
  • Resume playback. Store the last position per user and video, written every ~10–30 seconds from the client; a key-value store is enough.
  • Thumbnails and previews are small images served by the CDN, cached aggressively.
  • Deletion and takedown. Mark deleted in metadata (immediate effect on the API), purge CDN objects, then delete files in the background.

9. Bottlenecks, scaling and failure

  • Transcode backlog after a surge of uploads: autoscale workers on queue depth; show "processing" and let low-resolution renditions publish first.
  • Origin load during a viral spike of a new video: pre-warm the CDN, use an origin shield, and rely on request collapsing (many simultaneous misses become one origin fetch).
  • Storage cost: tier old and rarely watched videos to cheaper storage; drop unused high renditions for cold videos and re-create on demand.
  • Regional failure: replicate object storage across regions; multi-region CDN.
  • Failure cases: corrupt upload (validate and mark FAILED with a reason); worker crash (retry the chunk); manifest published before all segments exist (publish the manifest last).
  • Monitoring: start-up time, rebuffer ratio, CDN hit ratio, transcode queue lag and failure rate, bytes served per region.

Follow-up questions

  • How would you support live streaming? (Ingest by RTMP/WebRTC, near-real-time packaging into short segments, low-latency HLS, a very different latency budget.)
  • Why short segments? (Faster adaptation and seeking vs more requests and manifest overhead; 2–6 s is a common range.)
  • How do you reduce start-up time? (Start at a low rendition, small first segments, CDN proximity, preload the manifest.)
  • How do you protect paid content? (Signed URLs, token auth, DRM licences.)
  • How would you detect duplicate or copyrighted uploads? (Content fingerprinting at the validation step.)

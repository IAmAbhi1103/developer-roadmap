Joins: INNER, LEFT, RIGHT and FULL

A join combines rows from two tables where a condition matches, usually "foreign key equals primary key". INNER JOIN keeps only the matches; LEFT, RIGHT and FULL joins also keep the rows that found no partner and fill the gap with NULL.

Why joins exist

Good database design stores each fact once: an employee row holds dept_id = 1, and only the departments table knows that 1 means Engineering in Bengaluru. To show "Priya works in Engineering" you must put the two tables back together at query time. That is a join. Joins are the reason relational databases split data into tables without losing anything, and they are the single most tested SQL topic in interviews.

How an inner join works

Conceptually, the database takes every row of the left table and looks for rows of the right table where the ON condition is true. Each matching pair becomes one output row. Rows without a partner disappear. (Internally the database picks a smart method, such as a hash join or an index lookup. The result is the same as this simple picture.)

SELECT e.first_name, d.name AS department
FROM employees AS e
INNER JOIN departments AS d ON d.dept_id = e.dept_id
ORDER BY d.dept_id, e.emp_id;
 first_name | department
------------+-------------
 Vikram     | Engineering
 Priya      | Engineering
 Rahul      | Engineering
 Arjun      | Engineering
 Divya      | Engineering
 Ananya     | Sales
 Karan      | Sales
 Neha       | Sales
 Sunita     | HR
 Farhan     | HR
 Isha       | HR
(11 rows)

Twelve employees went in but only eleven came out: Meera has dept_id = NULL, and NULL equals nothing, so she has no partner. Marketing is missing too, since nobody works there. JOIN alone means INNER JOIN. Use table aliases (e, d) and qualify every column; without them, a column present in both tables fails with ERROR: column reference "dept_id" is ambiguous.

The four types side by side

LEFT JOIN

A left outer join (OUTER is optional) keeps every row of the left table. When there is no match, the right table's columns are NULL. Use it when the left table is the "main" thing and the right table is optional information.

SELECT e.first_name, d.name AS department
FROM employees AS e
LEFT JOIN departments AS d ON d.dept_id = e.dept_id
ORDER BY e.emp_id;
 first_name | department
------------+-------------
 Meera      | (null)
 Vikram     | Engineering
 Ananya     | Sales
 Sunita     | HR
 Priya      | Engineering
 Rahul      | Engineering
 Arjun      | Engineering
 Divya      | Engineering
 Karan      | Sales
 Neha       | Sales
 Farhan     | HR
 Isha       | HR
(12 rows)

Meera is back, with a NULL department. A left join always returns at least as many rows as the left table has.

RIGHT JOIN

A right join is a left join seen in a mirror: all rows of the right table are kept. Departments, including empty ones:

SELECT d.name AS department, e.first_name
FROM employees AS e
RIGHT JOIN departments AS d ON d.dept_id = e.dept_id
ORDER BY d.dept_id, e.emp_id;
  department  | first_name
-------------+------------
 Engineering | Vikram
 Engineering | Priya
 Engineering | Rahul
 Engineering | Arjun
 Engineering | Divya
 Sales       | Ananya
 Sales       | Karan
 Sales       | Neha
 HR          | Sunita
 HR          | Farhan
 HR          | Isha
 Marketing   | (null)
(12 rows)

Now Marketing appears with no employee, and Meera is gone. Most teams never write RIGHT JOIN: swap the table order and use LEFT JOIN, so every join in a query reads in the same direction. The query above equals FROM departments d LEFT JOIN employees e ON e.dept_id = d.dept_id.

FULL JOIN

A full outer join keeps unmatched rows from both sides: Meera (no department) and Marketing (no employees).

SELECT e.first_name, d.name AS department
FROM employees AS e
FULL JOIN departments AS d ON d.dept_id = e.dept_id
ORDER BY d.dept_id, e.emp_id;
 first_name | department
------------+-------------
 Vikram     | Engineering
 Priya      | Engineering
 Rahul      | Engineering
 Arjun      | Engineering
 Divya      | Engineering
 Ananya     | Sales
 Karan      | Sales
 Neha       | Sales
 Sunita     | HR
 Farhan     | HR
 Isha       | HR
 (null)     | Marketing
 Meera      | (null)
(13 rows)

13 = 11 matches + 1 employee without department + 1 department without employees. MySQL has no FULL JOIN. Simulate it with LEFT JOIN … UNION ALL … RIGHT JOIN … WHERE left.id IS NULL, or UNION of a left and a right join. Full joins are used for reconciliation: "what is in system A but not B, and the reverse".

Joining on more than equality

The ON condition can be any boolean expression, not only a foreign key. Several conditions are joined with AND, and a range join is legal (ON o.order_date BETWEEN p.start_date AND p.end_date). A join on a foreign key is by far the most common, and gets an index (Module 5).

Rule: pick the join type by asking "which rows must survive?"

If a row without a partner must still appear, the table that holds that row is on the preserved side of an outer join. If it must disappear, use an inner join. Wrong choice = missing rows (inner when you needed outer) or extra NULL rows.

Mistake: forgetting the ON condition

FROM employees e, departments d with no WHERE (the old comma style) or a typo in ON produces every combination: 12 × 4 = 48 rows. A query that returns "too many rows" almost always has a missing or wrong join condition. Always use explicit JOIN … ON.

Edge cases

  • NULL never joins to NULL. Two NULL keys do not match each other. Use ON a.x IS NOT DISTINCT FROM b.x if you want them to.
  • If the right side has several matches, the left row repeats once per match (one department row for each of its employees). That is correct, and a source of trouble when you aggregate afterwards (Join traps).
  • Joining INT to VARCHAR fails in PostgreSQL (operator does not exist: integer = character varying); cast one side.

Interview angle

Draw the Venn diagram, then give rows from real tables: "inner returns 11, left 12, full 13 because of Meera and Marketing". Be ready for "difference between inner and outer join", "what does a left join return when there is no match" (NULLs), and "why do you avoid right join" (readability, same as swapped left).

Self joins, cross joins and joining three or more tables

A self join joins a table to itself (employee to manager), a cross join pairs every row with every row (all combinations), and a query can chain as many joins as it needs. USING shortens equal-name joins; avoid NATURAL JOIN.

Self join: employee and manager

A table can reference itself: employees.manager_id holds the emp_id of another employee. To show both names in one row, use the table twice with two different aliases. Think of them as two copies: e is the employee, m is the manager.

SELECT e.emp_id,
       e.first_name AS employee,
       m.first_name AS manager
FROM employees AS e
LEFT JOIN employees AS m ON m.emp_id = e.manager_id
ORDER BY e.emp_id;
 emp_id | employee | manager
--------+----------+---------
      1 | Meera    | (null)
      2 | Vikram   | Meera
      3 | Ananya   | Meera
      4 | Sunita   | Meera
      5 | Priya    | Vikram
      6 | Rahul    | Priya
      7 | Arjun    | Priya
      8 | Divya    | Priya
      9 | Karan    | Ananya
     10 | Neha     | Ananya
     11 | Farhan   | Sunita
     12 | Isha     | Sunita
(12 rows)

It is a LEFT JOIN because the CEO has no manager; an inner join would drop Meera. Reverse the direction to count direct reports:

SELECT m.first_name AS manager, COUNT(*) AS direct_reports
FROM employees AS e
JOIN employees AS m ON m.emp_id = e.manager_id
GROUP BY m.emp_id, m.first_name
ORDER BY direct_reports DESC, manager;
 manager | direct_reports
---------+----------------
 Meera   |              3
 Priya   |              3
 Ananya  |              2
 Sunita  |              2
 Vikram  |              1
(5 rows)

A self join also finds pairs inside one group. The condition a.emp_id < b.emp_id keeps each pair once and removes pairs of a person with themselves:

SELECT a.first_name AS person_1, b.first_name AS person_2
FROM employees AS a
JOIN employees AS b ON a.dept_id = b.dept_id AND a.emp_id < b.emp_id
WHERE a.dept_id = 3
ORDER BY a.emp_id, b.emp_id;
 person_1 | person_2
----------+----------
 Sunita   | Farhan
 Sunita   | Isha
 Farhan   | Isha
(3 rows)

Self joins only follow one level. For the whole chain of command (manager of manager of …) you need a recursive query: Recursive CTEs.

Cross join: every combination

A cross join has no ON. It returns the Cartesian product: every row of A with every row of B, so A × B rows. That is a bug when it happens by accident and a tool when it is intended: generating a grid of combinations (every product with every size, every employee with every month for a leave plan, every day with every department for a report that shows zeros).

SELECT d.name AS department, q.quarter
FROM departments AS d
CROSS JOIN (VALUES ('Q1'), ('Q2')) AS q(quarter)
WHERE d.dept_id <= 2
ORDER BY d.dept_id, q.quarter;
 department | quarter
-------------+---------
 Engineering | Q1
 Engineering | Q2
 Sales       | Q1
 Sales       | Q2
(4 rows)

The size explodes: employees CROSS JOIN departments is 12 × 4 = 48 rows; two tables of a million rows give a trillion. A forgotten join condition is a cross join in disguise. The comma form FROM a, b is also a cross join, which is one more reason to write joins explicitly.

Joining three or more tables

Joins chain: each JOIN adds one table to the result built so far. Follow the foreign keys in the diagram of the sample database. Customer, order, line and product for the first two orders:

SELECT c.name AS customer, o.order_id, o.order_date,
       p.name AS product, oi.quantity, oi.unit_price
FROM customers   AS c
JOIN orders      AS o  ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id   = o.order_id
JOIN products    AS p  ON p.product_id  = oi.product_id
WHERE o.order_id <= 2
ORDER BY o.order_id, p.product_id;
  customer   | order_id | order_date |       product       | quantity | unit_price
-------------+----------+------------+---------------------+----------+------------
 Aarav Shah  |        1 | 2026-01-05 | Wireless Mouse      |        1 |     799.00
 Aarav Shah  |        1 | 2026-01-05 | Notebook A5         |        3 |     120.00
 Diya Kapoor |        2 | 2026-01-18 | Mechanical Keyboard |        1 |    3499.00
 Diya Kapoor |        2 | 2026-01-18 | USB-C Cable         |        2 |     299.00
(4 rows)

Tips: join in a logical chain, put each ON next to its join, and mix join types only on purpose. If you use LEFT JOIN in the middle, later joins on its columns should also be LEFT JOIN, otherwise an inner join silently removes the NULL-extended rows again. A many-to-many link goes through the junction table: employees to projects needs employee_projects in the middle.

SELECT p.name AS project, e.first_name, ep.role
FROM projects AS p
JOIN employee_projects AS ep ON ep.project_id = p.project_id
JOIN employees         AS e  ON e.emp_id      = ep.emp_id
WHERE p.project_id = 1
ORDER BY ep.hours_per_week DESC;
      project      | first_name |   role
-------------------+------------+-----------
 Payments Platform | Rahul      | Developer
 Payments Platform | Priya      | Lead
 Payments Platform | Arjun      | Developer
(3 rows)

ON, USING and NATURAL JOIN

USING (col) is shorthand when both tables have a column with the same name: JOIN departments d USING (dept_id) equals ON d.dept_id = e.dept_id, and the result shows dept_id once. It does not work when names differ (manager_id to emp_id).

NATURAL JOIN joins on all columns that have the same name, automatically. Avoid it: it changes meaning when someone adds a column. See what happens when departments and projects, which both have dept_id and name, are joined naturally:

SELECT COUNT(*) FROM departments NATURAL JOIN projects;      -- joins on dept_id AND name
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM departments d JOIN projects p USING (dept_id);
 count
-------
     0
(1 row)

 count
-------
     5
(1 row)

The natural join compares department names with project names and finds nothing; the explicit join finds all 5 projects.

Convention: alias well, qualify always

Short, meaningful aliases (e and m for employee and manager, not t1 and t2), and every column written as alias.column. In a self join the aliases are the only way to tell the two copies apart.

Mistake: inner join in a self join drops the top of the tree

The CEO has no manager, so JOIN employees m removes her. Use LEFT JOIN whenever "no parent" is a valid state, and the same goes for any optional foreign key.

Edge cases

  • Cross join with an empty table returns zero rows (A × 0 = 0).
  • A self join with ON a.dept_id = b.dept_id and no inequality also pairs each person with themselves and returns both (A,B) and (B,A).
  • In MySQL CROSS JOIN and JOIN without ON are the same thing; in PostgreSQL JOIN without ON is a syntax error.

Interview angle

"Show each employee with the manager's name" is the standard self-join question; the follow-up is "what about the CEO?" (left join). "When would you use a cross join?" (generating combinations, calendars with zeros) and "what is the result size of A cross join B?" (|A| × |B|).

Join traps: LEFT JOIN filters, anti-joins, NOT IN and row multiplication

Four mistakes cause most wrong join results: a WHERE that cancels a LEFT JOIN, counting with COUNT(*) after an outer join, NOT IN meeting a NULL, and a join that multiplies rows before you sum.

Trap 1: a WHERE condition turns LEFT JOIN into INNER JOIN

Goal: list every customer with their delivered orders; customers with none should still appear. Compare two versions:

Wrong: filter in WHERE

SELECT c.name, o.order_id
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE o.status = 'DELIVERED'
ORDER BY c.customer_id, o.order_id;

Right: filter in ON

SELECT c.name, o.order_id
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
                  AND o.status = 'DELIVERED'
ORDER BY c.customer_id, o.order_id;
    name     | order_id
-------------+----------
 Aarav Shah  |        1
 Aarav Shah  |        3
 Diya Kapoor |        2
 Kabir Singh |        6
 Ishita Joshi |       5
(5 rows)
     name     | order_id
--------------+----------
 Aarav Shah   |        1
 Aarav Shah   |        3
 Diya Kapoor  |        2
 Kabir Singh  |        6
 Ishita Joshi |        5
 Rohan Das    |   (null)
 Sara Khan    |   (null)
 Tanvi Desai  |   (null)
(8 rows)

Why? The join builds the rows first: Rohan, Sara and Tanvi (and orders that are not delivered) appear with o.status = NULL. Then WHERE o.status = 'DELIVERED' removes every row where the status is NULL or something else, including the NULL-extended rows. The left join is now an inner join in disguise. Rule: a condition on the optional (right) table belongs in ON; conditions on the preserved (left) table belong in WHERE. The exception is a deliberate WHERE o.order_id IS NULL, the anti-join below.

Trap 2: counting after an outer join

Headcount per department including empty ones:

SELECT d.name,
       COUNT(*)        AS wrong_count,
       COUNT(e.emp_id) AS headcount
FROM departments d
LEFT JOIN employees e ON e.dept_id = d.dept_id
GROUP BY d.dept_id, d.name
ORDER BY d.dept_id;
    name     | wrong_count | headcount
-------------+-------------+-----------
 Engineering |           5 |         5
 Sales       |           3 |         3
 HR          |           3 |         3
 Marketing   |           1 |         0
(4 rows)

Marketing has one row in the join result (the NULL-extended one), so COUNT(*) says 1. Count a column of the optional table; COUNT(e.emp_id) skips NULLs and gives 0. The same applies per customer: COUNT(o.order_id) gives Sara and Tanvi 0 orders.

Anti-joins: rows with no match

"Customers who never ordered" is an anti-join. There are three ways, and they should give the same answer when there are no NULLs:

-- 1. LEFT JOIN ... IS NULL
SELECT c.customer_id, c.name
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
WHERE o.order_id IS NULL;

-- 2. NOT EXISTS (usually the safest)
SELECT c.customer_id, c.name
FROM customers c
WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM orders o WHERE o.customer_id = c.customer_id);

-- 3. NOT IN (works only while the subquery has no NULLs)
SELECT customer_id, name
FROM customers
WHERE customer_id NOT IN (SELECT customer_id FROM orders);
 customer_id |    name
-------------+-------------
           6 | Sara Khan
           7 | Tanvi Desai
(2 rows)

With the LEFT JOIN … IS NULL form, test a column that cannot be NULL in a real row, such as the right table's primary key (o.order_id). PostgreSQL turns both the first and second form into the same anti join plan, so choose for clarity. Many teams prefer NOT EXISTS.

Trap 3: NOT IN and NULL

Which employees are not managers? manager_id contains NULL (Meera has no manager), and that breaks NOT IN.

Wrong: returns nothing

SELECT first_name FROM employees
WHERE emp_id NOT IN (SELECT manager_id FROM employees);
-- (0 rows)

Right: NOT EXISTS (or filter NULLs)

SELECT e.first_name FROM employees e
WHERE NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM employees x
                  WHERE x.manager_id = e.emp_id)
ORDER BY e.emp_id;
 first_name
------------
 Rahul
 Arjun
 Divya
 Karan
 Neha
 Farhan
 Isha
(7 rows)

The reason: 6 NOT IN (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, NULL) expands to 6 <> 1 AND … AND 6 <> NULL, and the last test is unknown, so the whole condition can never be true (see NULL and three-valued logic). The other fix is WHERE manager_id IS NOT NULL inside the subquery.

Trap 4: row multiplication (fan-out)

A join with a one-to-many relationship repeats the "one" side. If you then sum a column of the "one" side, it is counted many times. Total salary of the people who work on projects:

SELECT SUM(e.salary) AS wrong_total
FROM employees e
JOIN employee_projects ep ON ep.emp_id = e.emp_id;

SELECT SUM(salary) AS correct_total
FROM employees
WHERE emp_id IN (SELECT emp_id FROM employee_projects);
 wrong_total
-------------
  1330000.00
(1 row)

 correct_total
---------------
    1070000.00
(1 row)

Priya and Arjun are on two projects each, so their salaries were added twice (150,000 + 110,000 = 260,000 extra). Spot it by comparing row counts: 9 people, 11 joined rows. Fixes: aggregate before joining (in a subquery or CTE), use EXISTS/IN when you only need to test existence, or SUM(DISTINCT …) carefully (it also merges different people with equal salaries, so it is rarely right). The general rule is to know the cardinality of each join: one-to-one, one-to-many or many-to-many.

Rule: for a LEFT JOIN, filter the right table in ON and count a right-table column

Put conditions about the optional side in ON. Use COUNT(right.id), never COUNT(*). Use IS NULL on the right side only to find non-matches.

Tip: check row counts at every step

Run SELECT COUNT(*) after each added join. If the count jumps unexpectedly, a join is multiplying rows. Compare COUNT(*) with COUNT(DISTINCT e.emp_id) to see the duplication.

In real projects

A Spring Data JPA query SELECT d FROM Department d LEFT JOIN d.employees e WHERE e.salary > 1 has the same trap as Trap 1. And Hibernate's JOIN FETCH of a collection repeats the parent row once per child, which is the fan-out of Trap 4. Understanding the SQL makes both easy to debug.

Interview angle

"Find customers who never ordered" (three ways) and "why does NOT IN return no rows?" are standard. A strong answer names the NULL rule, shows NOT EXISTS, and mentions the left-join trap with WHERE vs ON. For fan-out, say "I check the join cardinality and aggregate before joining".
